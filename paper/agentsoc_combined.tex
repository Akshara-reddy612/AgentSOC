\title{Securing Autonomous Defense: Contamination and Memory Poisoning in a Learning Agentic SOC}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{\todo{authors (team)}}\IEEEauthorblockA{Dept. of CSE, KMIT, Hyderabad, India}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
An LLM-driven security operations centre (SOC) has two attack surfaces. Its model reads log text that an attacker
can write, and its memory learns what is normal from what it sees and from its analysts. We build the full pipeline
of an agentic SOC: LLM hypotheses, structural checks against a knowledge graph, response ranking, guardrails, a
learning Knowledge Store behind a learning gate, an outcome monitor and an analyst review loop. We evaluate both
surfaces, on 1,000 clean/contaminated alert pairs built from GUIDE and on four weeks of labelled telemetry from
DEDALE, a testbed company with an APT-style intrusion.
Text that hijacks an undefended LLM agent on 29.6\% of pairs suppresses the pipeline's response on 0.9\%, and no
misdirected action is executed. On DEDALE, attacker text gets a few attack cases dismissed, but the learning
gate keeps every one of them from being learned. Learning the benign weeks cuts analyst reviews from 6,095 to 65
per day while every step of the attack still reaches an analyst; continuing to learn without a gate learns the
attacker. Analyst mistakes are the larger poisoning route,
and retracting what was learned on a host once it is confirmed compromised closes it.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
security operations centre, large language models, prompt injection, memory poisoning, alert triage
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
SOC analysts face more alerts than they can investigate, and most of them turn out to be benign
activity~\cite{alahmadi}. Agentic SOC frameworks respond by putting a large language model (LLM) in the loop: it
reads an alert and its evidence, reasons about what the attacker may be doing, and proposes a response.
AgentSOC~\cite{agentsoc}, for example, has an LLM generate ATT\&CK hypotheses~\cite{attack}, checks them against
the structure of the enterprise and ranks containment actions by their effect and business impact. To reduce the
alert load further, such a system must also learn what is normal, so that routine activity stops raising alerts.

That makes the system attackable twice. First, much of what the LLM reads is written by the attacker: command lines,
script text, file names and the raw lines of their own activity as the sensors record them. Text in that evidence
can try to steer the model, as in indirect prompt injection~\cite{greshake}: it can claim that the activity is
approved maintenance, or name another host as the culprit. Second, a store that learns what is normal can be
taught. An attacker who is seen often enough, whose case is dismissed, or whom an analyst wrongly clears becomes
routine, and from then on their activity raises no alert at all. The first attack works once; the second lasts.

This paper evaluates both surfaces in one pipeline. The LLM's output is treated as advisory. Its hypotheses are
checked against trusted structured fields and an environment model before any response is ranked; guardrails
decide what may run without an analyst; and only structured fields, never text, are learned, behind a learning gate
that holds back anything near an escalation. Analysts' verdicts pass through an inbox that the loop applies, and a
host confirmed compromised has its recent learning retracted. We measure the pipeline on a contamination corpus
built from GUIDE~\cite{guide} and on DEDALE~\cite{dedale}: four weeks of labelled host and network telemetry from a
testbed company, with an APT-style intrusion. Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1:} attacker-written text does not steer the pipeline's actions. It hijacks an undefended LLM agent
on 29.6\% of alert pairs but suppresses the pipeline's response on 0.9\%, with no misdirected action executed.
On DEDALE, through the full loop, it teaches the store none of the attacker's patterns.
\item \textbf{C2:} a store that learns the benign weeks cuts analyst reviews from 6,095 to 65 per day on DEDALE's
host logs while every step of the attack still reaches review; with correct analyst verdicts it learns further,
to 31--34 per day.
\item \textbf{C3:} memory poisoning is real. Without the gate the store learns the attacker, and an analyst who
misses 20\% of attacks silences over a third of the attack events that would otherwise alert. Retracting what was
learned on a host once it is confirmed compromised removes the effect.
\item \textbf{C4:} on DEDALE the LLM's contribution is modest, and a closed-world feasibility check dismissed two
attack cases, which led us to treat hosts without telemetry as unknown.
\item \textbf{C5:} the analyst's response time sets the workload and the time to containment, not detection.
\end{itemize}
All numbers are reproducible offline from recorded LLM outputs.

\section{Background and Related Work}
% team: check the positioning sentences against your own literature search.
\textbf{Agentic SOCs.} AgentSOC~\cite{agentsoc} combines a Narrative Counterfactual Engine (NCE) that generates
multiple ATT\&CK hypotheses with an LLM, a Structural Simulation Engine (SSE) that checks them against enterprise
topology, identity and policy, and a Risk Scoring and Evaluation Module (RSEM) that ranks responses; it was
evaluated conceptually and with a proof of concept on authentication data. We build on its three engines and add
the loop around them: learning, gating, monitoring and analyst review. Microsoft's guided response system~\cite{guide}
recommends investigation, triage and remediation actions to SOC analysts; its authors released GUIDE, a large set of
real incidents with analyst triage labels, from which our contamination corpus is built.

\textbf{Prompt injection.} Text that a model processes as data can override its instructions~\cite{perez}; when the
text comes from documents, web pages or tools the application retrieves, the attack is indirect~\cite{greshake}.
Liu et al.~\cite{liu} formalise these attacks and benchmark defences. Defences work at several levels: marking the
provenance of untrusted input in the prompt~\cite{spotlighting}, training models to follow instructions only from a
separate trusted channel~\cite{struq}, or building a system layer that keeps untrusted data out of an agent's
control flow~\cite{camel}. A SOC is an unusually direct case: the attacker writes the very evidence the model must
read. Our defences are of the system kind; they bound what the model's output can cause rather than whether the
model is fooled.

\textbf{Poisoning what a system learns.} Detectors that learn normal behaviour can be poisoned through their
training data so that later attacks pass unnoticed~\cite{antidote}. LLM agents inherit the problem through their
memory and knowledge bases: a few poisoned entries can steer an agent's actions~\cite{agentpoison} or a
retrieval-augmented model's answers~\cite{poisonedrag}. Machine unlearning~\cite{unlearning} removes the influence
of specific training data, motivated in part by such pollution. Our learning gate limits what can enter the store,
and unlearning on confirmed compromise is a targeted, provenance-based form of it.

\textbf{Alert triage.} Analysts validate large numbers of alarms, most of them false or benign, which leads to
fatigue and desensitisation~\cite{alahmadi}. Learning routine activity is the main lever on that workload, which
is why the memory surface matters as much as the model surface.

These lines of work mostly study one surface at a time. Here both are evaluated in one SOC pipeline, on labelled
testbed telemetry, with the analyst included as a route for poisoning.

\section{Threat Model}
The attacker controls the free text of their own activity as sensors record it: command lines, script text, raw
log lines and the names they give their tools. They do not control structured sensor fields (host, account,
parent process, image path as recorded, addresses), the asset inventory, the learning gate, or the analyst.
Their goals are \emph{suppression} (their activity is dismissed, or learned as routine so that it stops raising
alerts) and \emph{misdirection} (containment aimed at the wrong host or account). Tampering with structured
fields at the sensor is out of scope for the defences; we measure its effect separately (Section~\ref{sec:c1}).

Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the system, which extends AgentSOC~\cite{agentsoc}. For each new case, the Narrative Counterfactual Engine (NCE), an LLM, proposes ATT\&CK~\cite{attack} hypotheses from the alert.
The Structural Simulation Engine (SSE) checks each against a knowledge graph of hosts, accounts, access and network
reachability; entity binding compares the entities a hypothesis names with the alert's trusted fields; RSEM ranks
responses by containment and business impact; guardrails decide what may run automatically. Evidence Risk
Assessment (ERA) scores the attacker-writable text with pattern, embedding-similarity, approval-claim and
split-field detectors; a medium or high score sends the case to an analyst, and ERA never dismisses one. Around
this single-alert pipeline runs the loop:
\begin{itemize}
\item a \emph{triage gate} skips patterns the store has learned as routine and reuses decisions for unchanged cases;
\item a \emph{learning Knowledge Store} learns structured patterns only (never text; scripts as fingerprints), from
  a benign training window and afterwards only behind a \emph{learning gate}: a dismissed case is learned after a
  delay, and only if nothing involving its endpoints was escalated meanwhile;
\item an \emph{outcome monitor} watches the targets of executed (simulated) responses and escalates continued activity;
\item an \emph{analyst review loop}: analysts' verdicts and approvals enter an inbox that the loop applies (the store
  keeps a single writer); containment of critical hosts needs two analysts; and when a host is confirmed
  compromised, what was learned on it in the previous 7 days is retracted and nothing new is learned on it.
\end{itemize}
SSE treats hosts without telemetry as unknown, not as impossible targets (open world, Section~\ref{sec:c4}).

\section{Evaluation Setup}
\textbf{GUIDE corpus v2.} 500 development and 500 held-out pairs built from Microsoft GUIDE incidents~\cite{guide}: each pair is
a clean alert and a contaminated twin that differs only at the injection site, across 10 payload families
(suppression and identity steering), with entities from a synthetic enterprise inventory. Each alert goes through
the single-alert pipeline (NCE, SSE with entity binding, RSEM, guardrails and ERA) on its own; the loop around it
is evaluated on DEDALE.

\textbf{DEDALE~\cite{dedale}.} Four weeks of host and network logs recorded in a testbed that emulates a small
company (30 Windows clients, servers, a DMZ), with an APT-style intrusion and exact labels of the malicious host
events on the two attacked clients. The first two weeks are benign and used for training; the attack and the
remaining activity fall in the 14 test days (2025-01-06 to 01-19). Detection on host logs comes from the store
itself: six detectors turn events into structured patterns (process lineage, named pipes, executable file writes,
PowerShell script fingerprints, logons, LSASS access), and an event whose pattern the store has not learned becomes
an alert. Network alerts come from Suricata. The loop runs without ERA.

\textbf{Conditions.} Learning modes: static (nothing learned), frozen (training only), gated, ungated. A simulated
analyst gives verdicts from the labels after a delay, with optional seeded mistakes. NCE is
\texttt{gemini-3.1-flash-lite} (and \texttt{gpt-oss-20b} on the GUIDE development set). It proposes techniques from
a fixed list that SSE can check: 7 on GUIDE, 18 on DEDALE's host logs. On DEDALE, NCE is off unless stated (C2,
C3, C5), so every case the store cannot explain goes to review; it is on in E1 and C4. Live outputs are recorded
and replayed, so every number is reproducible offline.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Contamination (C1)}\label{sec:c1}
Table~\ref{tab:c1} compares responses on clean and contaminated twins. Contamination suppressed a response the clean
twin received in 4 of 428 (development) and 3 of 437 (held-out) pairs; the opposite flips, apart from 12 and 10
caused by ERA escalation, were 4 and 0 (McNemar $p=1.0$ and $0.25$). ERA flagged 65 and 56 of the 500
contaminated alerts and no clean one; a flagged alert cannot be suppressed. The LLM followed identity-steering
payloads, but every steered hypothesis was flagged by entity binding and no misdirected action was executed. On
the same 500 development pairs, an undefended triage agent (the same LLM, given the alert's fields and asked for a
verdict) was hijacked into dismissing 130 of the 439 contaminated alerts whose clean twin it did not dismiss
(29.6\%, 95\% CI 25.5--34.0\%, $p=6.5\times10^{-17}$), against 4 of 428 for the pipeline. The two measures differ
in kind, the agent's own verdict against the pipeline's decision, and we did not evaluate a prompt-level
defence~\cite{spotlighting,struq} as a further baseline. With a second model,
\texttt{gpt-oss-20b}, as the NCE, the pipeline's response was suppressed on 10 of 437 development pairs (2.3\%, 95\%
CI 1.2--4.2\%) against 5 opposite flips ($p=0.3$), and again no misdirected action was executed.

\textbf{Through the full loop on DEDALE (E1).} We appended attacker text to the command lines of the attacker's own
84 process events (38 cases expose it to NCE), in three variants: a maintenance claim, a fake SOC approval, and an

\subsection{The learning store (C2)}
Table~\ref{tab:c2}: without learning, every unseen pattern is a case, 6,095 reviews per day on host logs. Learning
the training weeks cuts this to 65 per day. It also silences 73 of the 473 labelled attack events that alert when
nothing is learned, because their patterns occur in benign activity too; every step of the attack still reaches
review, as early as without learning (initial access 1.2 min after it began, lateral movement after 28.5 min).
Gated learning adds nothing here (gated equals frozen): the gate learns only cases dismissed without escalation,
and with NCE and the analyst off nothing is dismissed. Learning without the gate cuts further, to 27.4, but only
by learning the attacker: 38 attack cases' patterns became routine and only 99 of the 400 attack events raised
alerts. On network alerts, learning cuts 552 to 26 reviews per day. Suricata alerted on only one of the 84
labelled attack flows at DEDALE's company router, the dropper download; without the gate the store learned that
download as routine, and with the gate it learned nothing of the attack. Fig.~\ref{fig:days} shows the workload
day by day.

\subsection{Memory poisoning through the analyst (C3)}
Analyst verdicts bypass the gate by design. A simulated analyst missing 20\% of attacks taught the store 20 attack
cases' patterns, and only 256 of 400 attack events still raised alerts (Table~\ref{tab:c3}). Unlearning on
confirmed compromise removes the effect: with the same mistakes, nothing was learned and all 400 events alerted.
It costs 10\% more reviews with a perfect analyst (30.9 to 34.1 per day) and makes false alarms more expensive
(31.4 to 44.6 per day at 5\% false alarms), because a false confirmation stops learning on a healthy host.

\subsection{What the LLM adds (C4)}\label{sec:c4}
With NCE, reviews fall from 65.2 to 54.8 per day (16\%), by dismissing cases whose hypotheses are all structurally
infeasible. Under a closed-world check this dismissed 2 of the 72 attack cases: the attacker copied data from a
server the store has no telemetry for, and ``no record of access'' was read as ``impossible''. Treating hosts
without telemetry as unknown removed both misses at no cost (89 benign dismissals either way). We adopted this
open-world rule after seeing the two misses, on the same data, so its 0 of 72 is not an independent test. With an analyst
in the loop, NCE adds little (33.2 reviews per day with NCE, 34.1 without, both with unlearning).

\subsection{Operating the loop (C5)}
Detection does not depend on how fast analysts answer, but workload does: host reviews per day rise from 33.1 to
51.5 as the delay grows from 1 h to 72 h, because benign patterns are learned only when their verdict arrives.
On the network, the attacker's server stays reachable until the verdict: containment came 60 min to 72 h after the
first alert, equal to the delay (Fig.~\ref{fig:delay}).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
% Drafted by Claude 2026-10-19 from RESULTS.md; for the team to revise.
\textbf{Where the defence lives.} The LLM itself was not made robust. Acting alone, it was hijacked on 29.6\% of
the GUIDE pairs, and on DEDALE it named the steered host in 30--32 of 38 cases. What held was the structure around
it. Hypotheses are checked against the trusted fields and the environment model before any response is ranked. A
target the trusted fields do not support is flagged, not acted on. Only structured fields are ever learned. This is
the principle of recent designs that keep untrusted data out of an agent's control flow~\cite{camel}, applied to a
SOC: the LLM's output is advisory, and what it may cause is decided by components it cannot write to. Prompt-level
defences~\cite{spotlighting,struq} reduce how often a model follows injected text; our measurements concern what
happens when it does.

\textbf{Memory is the larger surface.} A system that learns what is normal is a classic poisoning
target~\cite{antidote}, and agents' memories and knowledge bases have the same weakness~\cite{agentpoison,poisonedrag}.
On DEDALE, learning without the gate cut the labelled attack events that raised alerts from 400 to 99; with the
gate, none was lost.
The weaker route is the analyst. Verdicts bypass the gate by design, and with a simulated analyst who missed 20\% of the
attacks, only 256 of the 400 still raised alerts. Unlearning on confirmed compromise closed this route: a targeted form of machine
unlearning~\cite{unlearning} that retracts what was recently learned on a host once the host is confirmed
compromised. It has two costs. It works only because each attacked host received at least one correct malicious
verdict. And it makes false confirmations expensive, raising reviews from 31.4 to 44.6 per day at 5\% false alarms.
The prototype lets a lead withdraw a wrong confirmation, which restores what it retracted; we did not evaluate this.

\textbf{Absence of telemetry is not impossibility.} The closed-world feasibility check dismissed 2 real attack cases,
because the store had no telemetry from the server the attacker copied data from. Any structural check of LLM
hypotheses inherits the coverage of its environment model. Hosts without telemetry must be treated as unknown, and
a deployment needs to know which hosts it cannot see.

\textbf{Workload.} Most of the reduction comes from learning the benign weeks: 6,095 to 65 reviews per day. Analyst verdicts feeding
the store take it to 34. The LLM cuts reviews by 16\% on its own, and by little once an analyst is in the loop
(34.1 to 33.2). On this data, the LLM's measurable effect on workload is modest. We did not measure its effect on
how fast or how accurately analysts decide, which is where its explanations would matter.

\textbf{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item One labelled dataset, from a testbed. Labels exist for two attacked Windows clients. Most labelled attack
events on them (DLL loads, ordinary file writes) are of kinds the host detectors do not cover. Suricata alerted on
1 of the 84 labelled attack flows, so the network results rest on very few attack cases.
\item Choices made on DEDALE itself: the host detectors were selected by measuring, on the test weeks, how often
benign and labelled attack events produce new patterns; the open-world rule followed the two misses
(Section~\ref{sec:c4}); and the prototype's review-queue order was tuned on this data and is not evaluated.
\item A simulated analyst: an oracle with random, per-case mistakes. Real analysts' mistakes are likely correlated
with how well the attacker blends in, which would make C3 harder.
\item One LLM on DEDALE (\texttt{gemini-3.1-flash-lite}). The second model, \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}, was run on the GUIDE
development set only (Table~\ref{tab:c1}).
\item GUIDE v2's entities, evidence and payloads are synthetic. E1 injects text only into command lines, with three
payload families.
\item Tampering with structured sensor fields is outside the defences (Section~\ref{sec:c1}).
\item All containment is simulated.
\item Each LLM condition was run once. Replaying the recorded outputs makes every number reproducible, but
run-to-run variation of the model is not measured.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What a deployment would need:}
\begin{itemize}
\item an asset inventory and environment model that are kept current, since SSE and entity binding are only as good
as they are;
\item known telemetry coverage per host;
\item a study with real analysts, who face alert fatigue~\cite{alahmadi};
\item a second dataset, to check that the detectors and the gate's settings (a 24\,h delay, and a hold while
related cases are escalated) transfer.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
An LLM-driven SOC can be attacked through what its model reads and through what its memory learns. In our
pipeline, the model was easy to steer, but its output was advisory: hypotheses were checked against trusted fields
and the environment model, steered targets were flagged, and no misdirected action ran. The memory was the larger
risk. Learning made the workload manageable (6,095 to 65 reviews per day on DEDALE's host logs) and, without a
gate, also learned the attacker. The gate held, and the weakest remaining route, wrong analyst verdicts, was closed
by retracting what was learned on a host once it was confirmed compromised. A deployment would need a maintained
environment model, known telemetry coverage, and evaluation with real analysts and on more than one dataset.
